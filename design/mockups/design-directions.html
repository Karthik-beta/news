Round 2: closer to the FT and The Economist

These three borrow how the FT and The Economist work, not their brand assets. Round 1 is further down for comparison.

What makes them look like themselves

One owned colour
The FT owns its pink paper. The Economist owns one red, used for the logo, topic lines and chart rules.
Bold serif headlines
Set tight and confident, with a plain sans-serif for charts, labels and small text.
A topic line
The Economist puts a short coloured line above each headline saying what the story is about.
A house chart style
Bold takeaway title, grey subtitle, light gridlines, direct labels, a small source line. The Economist adds a red rule and tag across the top.
Restraint
Almost no boxes, shadows or decoration around the data.

What we can't take: the FT's exact pink, The Economist's red box logo, and their fonts (Financier, Metric, Milo and Econ Sans are licensed). These directions use free fonts with a similar character.

DFT school

Warm tinted paper, black serif headlines and a clean grotesk for everything else. Petrol blue and claret are the working colours.

Why it could work

The closest to the FT's feel. Tinted paper is the single most recognisable thing about the FT, and Schibsted Grotesk was made for a news publisher.

What to watch

Any warm pink or peach paper will be read as copying the FT. This sand tint is as close as I'd go, and it may still be too close for a site that wants its own identity.

EEconomist school

Near-white paper and one crimson, used for the wordmark, the topic line and a rule across the top of every chart.

Why it could work

The red rule and tag are what make Economist charts recognisable from across a room. Literata is a bookish serif in the same spirit as The Economist's Milo, and Fira Sans plays the role of its chart font.

What to watch

Red reads as "loss" in finance. There's no tinted paper, so all the identity rests on the red. This crimson is deeper than The Economist's orange-red, but the family resemblance is strong.

FLedger, the FT way

Direction A's ledger green, used the way the FT and The Economist use their colours: a stronger paper tint, bold serif headlines, a topic line and a green rule across every chart.

Why it could work

You get their confidence and structure with a colour no news brand owns. Most of what makes a page feel like the FT or The Economist is structure, and this keeps all of it.

What to watch

Same as A: green means "up" in finance, so changes are always written out in words.

My pick from round 2: F, or E if you want red

F feels closest to the FT and The Economist while staying yours: the confidence comes from bold headlines, the topic line and the chart rule, and the colour is one nobody owns. E is the strongest if you love The Economist specifically, but it leans on a red that readers will connect with them.

D is the most FT-like, and that is its problem: a warm tinted paper will always look like a cousin of the FT.

Round 1

The first three directions, kept here for comparison.

ALedger

Pale ledger-green paper and deep green ink, like an accounts book or a banknote.

Why it could work

No major news brand owns green (FT is pink, The Economist is red), and green sits between money and science, the two ends of your coverage. Newsreader was drawn for reading news on screens. Public Sans is the US government's own typeface, a quiet nod to official sources.

What to watch

In finance, green means "up". Changes would always be written out ("up 2.0%") and never shown by green alone.

BBulletin

Cool grey-blue paper, Prussian-blue ink and one amber marker for the number that matters, like a central-bank bulletin.

Why it could work

The most technical and analytical of the three. The amber makes the key figure in every chart stand out at a glance. Sans-serif headlines over serif text is The Economist's own arrangement, so it still reads as serious journalism.

What to watch

Blue is the most common corporate colour, so the paper tint and the amber have to do the work of making it yours. Amber can also read as a warning.

CClaret

Lilac-grey paper, claret ink and a high-contrast display serif. The most editorial of the three, closest to a magazine.

Why it could work

The most distinctive at a glance. Claret is warm and serious, and no one in business news owns it. Libre Franklin descends from Franklin Gothic, a classic of American newspapers.

What to watch

Red-family colours read as "down" or "loss" in finance. The thin strokes of the headline serif need large sizes and can feel more fashion than analysis.

Specimen C

The same in every direction

  • Chart titles state the finding; a smaller line underneath says what is plotted and in which units.
  • Values are labelled on the chart directly, so there are no legends to decode.
  • One series or bar is highlighted in the signature colour; everything else stays muted.
  • A source line sits under every chart and figure group, and every number shows its exact source on hover or tap.
  • Changes are written in words ("up 2.0%"), so meaning never depends on colour alone.
  • No photos, gradients, shadows or animation. Rules and spacing do the structuring.
